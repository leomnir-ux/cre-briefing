# U.S. CRE Weekly Briefing, November 2 to November 8, 2026

## Market Summary

### The week in brief: Manhattan office leasing runs at its fastest pace since 2000
*Office led the news again, and this week the story was demand: Manhattan tenants are paying pre-pandemic rents while national office vacancy is still above 18%.*

The Real Deal reported on November 8 that Manhattan office leasing is on pace for its strongest year since 2000. Colliers data cited in the piece showed about **4.5 million square feet** leased in October, up more than 50% from September, and more than **37 million square feet** leased so far this year, with roughly **44 million** expected by year-end. Average asking rent in October was close to **$80 per square foot**, matching March 2020, and Class A asking rents passed **$90** in the third quarter, a record. Finance and law firms are doing most of the leasing, technology is a growing share, and AI companies alone leased more than 1 million square feet in Q3, almost all of it in Midtown South.

Supply is helping landlords. Apart from JPMorgan Chase’s headquarters, no major new tower has opened since One Vanderbilt in 2021, and the next wave is not expected until about 2030. Thirteen office-to-residential conversions are expected to take more than **7 million square feet** of office space off the market. Availability fell to just over 12% in October, the lowest level since 2020.

Colliers’ October report, published November 2, filled in the detail. Leasing rose **54.1%** from September and **24.7%** from October 2025. Average asking rent rose to **$79.47 per square foot**, from $78.36 in September and $75.18 a year earlier, and availability fell to **12.3%**, the tightest since August 2020. Sublet supply dropped to its lowest point in seven years, and Midtown led with 2.4 million square feet leased.

**My read:** Manhattan is now a landlord’s market for the best buildings, and the reason is as much missing supply as new demand. Nationally the picture is slower, with CBRE’s Q3 overall vacancy at 18.2%, so Manhattan’s numbers should not be read as a national office recovery.

**Sources:** [The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/national/2026/11/08/manhattans-office-comeback/), [Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2026/11/manhattan-office-leasing-october-2026-colliers-report/), [CBRE](https://www.cbre.com/insights/figures/q3-2026-us-office-market-report)

### Rates and the Fed: the 10-year slips below 5% after the Fed holds
*The Fed paused after its September increase, and long-term yields eased through the week.*

At its October 28 meeting, the Federal Open Market Committee held the federal funds target range at **3.75% to 4.00%**. The vote was 10 to 2, and both dissenters wanted another 25 basis point increase. The committee did not release new economic projections at that meeting. The hold followed the September increase that brought the range to its current level.

Long-term rates moved lower. Treasury data show the 10-year yield at **4.91% on November 2** and **4.95% on November 3**, then **4.90%** on November 4, **4.84%** on November 5 and **4.86%** on November 6. On October 29, it had closed at 4.98%. The 2-year yield fell from 4.61% on November 3 to 4.46% on November 5 before rising to 4.51% on November 6.

The inflation data behind the Fed’s caution remain uncomfortable. September consumer prices rose **3.7%** from a year earlier, with core prices up 2.6%, and producer prices rose **6.3%**, including a 1.3% monthly gain that was the largest since March 2022. The labor market is soft by comparison: payrolls rose by only 29,000 in September and unemployment ticked up to 4.2%.

**My read:** A 10-year yield in the high 4% range is a modest relief for fixed-rate borrowers, but two votes for a hike mean nobody should underwrite a rate cut. For a refinancing, the useful question is whether the deal works at today’s rates, not whether rates will fall.

### Credit and CMBS: distress is concentrated in older loans and weaker markets
*The national numbers are still at multi-year highs, and lenders are giving troubled loans less time.*

The latest national CMBS figures available to this briefing are for September. Delinquency rose 17 basis points to **8.02%**, the highest level since November 2020, and the special servicing rate rose to **11.49%**, the highest since February 2013. October’s national report was not available in sources I could open this week.

Maturities are adding pressure. Trepp data cited by Waterstone showed **$4.70 billion** of hard maturities across 118 CMBS loans in October, up 71.5% from September. Trepp expects **$76.6 billion** of hard maturities for all of 2026, with 39% of it falling in the fourth quarter.

Lenders are responding by shortening the runway. At Commercial Observer’s National Finance Forum on November 3, Nomura’s Larry Kravetz said lenders are much less willing to extend troubled multifamily loans, and that extensions are now measured in months rather than years. He expects more foreclosures and loan sales, and noted that many properties need capital work their owners cannot fund while debt service is barely covered.

**My read:** Delinquency is concentrated in older loans written at peak values. A heavy fourth-quarter maturity calendar combined with shorter extensions means more of those loans will be resolved through sales, recapitalizations or foreclosures in 2027.

**Sources:** [Multifamily Dive](https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/cmbs-servicing-federal-reserve-apartment-deliquency/833363/), [Multi-Housing News](https://www.multihousingnews.com/cmbs-delinquency-rates/), [Waterstone Defeasance](https://www.defeasanceservices.com/october-2026-market-update/), [Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2026/11/commercial-real-estate-distress-2026/)

### Capital and pricing: lenders are back, but values are still well below peak
*Commercial Observer’s fall finance forum on November 3 showed a market where debt is available and equity is the gap.*

Panelists at Commercial Observer’s National Finance Forum, held on Election Day, described lenders competing for deals while owners struggle with values. KBS’s Sondra Wenger said commercial property values are about **19% below their peaks** on average, and estimated that owners may need **$470 billion to $500 billion** of new equity across 2026 and 2027. Her view was that the problem sits in the capital stack rather than in the real estate itself, which creates an opening for investors who can fill refinancing gaps.

Lending volume supports that read. JLL’s Christopher Pratt said his group’s CMBS volume was up about 54% from a year earlier, debt fund lending about 70%, bank lending about 35% and life insurance company lending about 22%. Two-thirds of JLL’s deals over $200 million went to balance sheet lenders, and large loans were drawing about eight quoting lenders. Walker & Dunlop’s Adam Schwartz gave a less crowded picture, saying loans now draw two or three bids rather than five or six, and Flagstar’s Mark Silverstein said CMBS spreads are about 15 basis points wider on conduit loans and 30 basis points wider on CRE CLO loans than at their best levels.

The panel disagreed about how bad the coming cycle will be. Northwind’s Ran Eliasaf said higher rates and their effect on cap rates could create stress comparable to 2008, though he was not sure of the odds. Acore’s Eric Ramirez expected the reset to play out mostly in 2027, with office further along than multifamily. Starwood’s distressed fund XIII closed in July with more than **$10.2 billion** of commitments.

**My read:** Debt is easier to find than equity right now. When lenders size loans to today’s values and rates, an owner who bought or refinanced in 2021 often has to bring new cash, and that shortfall, rather than a lack of lenders, is what forces sales.

**Sources:** [Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2026/11/commercial-real-estate-distress-2026/)

### Industrial and retail: strong demand is turning into refinancings and trades
*Industrial owners are borrowing against better numbers, and open-air retail is changing hands at a wide range of prices.*

Industrial had the week’s largest trade. Adkisson Group and Los Angeles-based PCCP bought Northwoods Industrial Park in Houston, a 2.7 million-square-foot park of 64 buildings that is **98% occupied**, in what The Real Deal described as the largest single industrial park acquisition in Houston history. The price was not disclosed. In Miami-Dade County, Wells Fargo provided LBA Realty a **$133 million** refinancing on the 709,480-square-foot Dolphin Commerce Center, adding $83 million to the $50 million balance of its prior loan.

The demand behind those loans is real. CBRE’s Q3 report recorded **104.6 million square feet** of net absorption, the most since Q4 2022, and vacancy of **6.4%**. Net asking rent rose 3.6% to $11.16 per square foot, and CBRE projects industrial investment of $130.7 billion this year, up 15%.

Retail trades favored grocery-anchored and value-add centers. Midloch Investment Partners and Core Acquisitions bought Lamar Station Plaza in Lakewood, Colorado, home of Casa Bonita, for **$30.3 million**, or about $112 per square foot, with the 271,630-square-foot center 76% occupied. In the Chicago area, Crain’s data cited by The Real Deal put retail investment at about **$1.8 billion** so far in 2026, up roughly 39%, led by grocery-anchored and open-air centers.

**My read:** Industrial buyers and lenders are backing large, well-leased properties, from a 98% occupied Houston park to a cash-out refinancing in Miami. In retail, Lamar Station’s low price per square foot reflects its vacancy, and the buyers are paying for lease-up risk.

**Sources:** [The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/texas/2026/11/04/adkisson-lands-houstons-largest-industrial-campus-deal/), [Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2026/11/wells-fargo-dolphin-commerce-center-lba-realty/), [CBRE](https://www.cbre.com/insights/figures/q3-2026-us-industrial-and-logistics-market-report), [The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/national/denver/2026/11/05/chicago-buyers-grabs-casa-bonita-retail-center-near-denver/), [The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/chicago/2026/11/03/retail-investment-up-1-8b-due-to-grocery-anchored-shopping/)

### Hotels, senior housing and data centers: buyers keep paying for operating strength
*The operating businesses had good news, while data centers ran into local politics.*

Hotels posted a strong week. CoStar data for October 25 to 31, reported November 5, showed RevPAR up **9.7%** to $101.88, with occupancy up 4.3% and average daily rate up 5.2%. The World Series lifted Tampa, and conferences helped New Orleans post the largest RevPAR gain among the top 25 markets. Host Hotels & Resorts added two hotels for about **$374 million**: the leasehold on the 195-room 1 Hotel Brooklyn Bridge for $202 million and the 308-room Renaissance Dallas at Plano Legacy West for about $172 million. Host’s comparable RevPAR rose 8.8% in Q3.

In senior housing, Artemis Real Estate Partners bought Atria Newport Beach, a 168-unit community, from a Ventas affiliate for **$88.5 million**, about $526,785 per unit, with a life insurance company providing the financing.

Data centers had a busy week of financing and pushback. Newsletter summaries of DatacenterDynamics reporting said the four largest hyperscalers are raising more than **$131 billion** in debt and equity for AI buildout, including an Amazon loan facility of up to about $80 billion secured by certain purchased assets, likely AI hardware according to Value Add Pulse. Google confirmed in late October that it is studying about **1,000 acres** in Goochland County, Virginia, for a 1,200-megawatt campus, and four residents are suing the county over how it adopted the overlay district that makes the site a by-right use.

**My read:** In hotels and senior housing, buyers are paying for proven operating income. In data centers, money is not the constraint. Approvals, power and local politics are, and that risk sits with the landowner and developer rather than the tenant.

### Apartments: rents are steady, but lenders are losing patience
*CBRE’s Q3 data showed supply finally easing, while lenders described less room for troubled loans.*

CBRE’s Q3 multifamily report, published November 4, showed average monthly rent of **$2,268**, up 1% from a year earlier and 0.5% from Q2. Vacancy rose 40 basis points to **4.7%**, still below the long-term average of about 5%. Demand slowed sharply, with net absorption of **8,747 units** compared with 42,166 in Q2, but completions also fell 11% from a year earlier to 85,300 units, and CBRE expects construction to slow further. The number of markets with falling rents dropped to 21 from 33.

Credit is the weaker side. September multifamily CMBS delinquency rose 35 basis points to **8.04%**, and special servicing rose to 8.54%. At Commercial Observer’s forum, Nomura’s Larry Kravetz said lenders are much less willing to extend troubled apartment loans, and that extensions are now measured in months rather than years. He expects more foreclosures and loan sales, and noted that many properties need capital work their owners cannot fund while debt service is barely covered.

Investment volume looks better than it is. CBRE said the $69.6 billion AvalonBay and Equity Residential merger lifted Q3 investment activity, but excluding that deal, volume fell 8.7% from a year earlier. CBRE’s Kelli Carhart said the bid-ask gap remains a challenge even as fundamentals improve.

**My read:** Apartment fundamentals are past their worst, with peak supply behind the sector. The problem is the loans written at 2021 and 2022 values, and a shorter extension window means more of those properties will trade or be foreclosed in 2027.

**Sources:** [CBRE](https://www.cbre.com/press-releases/us-multifamily-rents-edge-higher-q3-2026-construction-slows), [Multifamily Dive](https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/cmbs-servicing-federal-reserve-apartment-deliquency/833363/), [Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2026/11/commercial-real-estate-distress-2026/)

## Office

**How office is performing:** Office is splitting more sharply than any other property type. In Manhattan, Colliers data showed October leasing up more than 50% from September and the market on pace for its best year since 2000. Average asking rent reached $79.47 per square foot in October, up from $75.18 a year earlier and in line with its pre-pandemic level, and availability fell to 12.3%, its lowest since August 2020. Sublet space fell to its lowest point in seven years. Class A asking rents topped $90 per square foot in Q3, and tenant improvement allowances hit a record above $90 per square foot in the first half, mainly on smaller, lower-rent deals.

National numbers are improving more slowly. CBRE’s Q3 report showed overall vacancy of 18.2% and prime vacancy of 11.9%, with 4.9 million square feet of net absorption. New supply is minimal, and in Manhattan, 13 conversions to residential are expected to remove more than 7 million square feet. Outside the strongest markets, the recovery is slower, and the gap between prime and overall vacancy shows tenants are concentrating in the best buildings.

**Capital markets read-through:** Lenders are willing to finance office buildings with strong leasing, as the 350 Park Avenue construction loan showed last month. [Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2026/10/ken-griffin-citadel-350-park-manhattan/) Older buildings with large vacancies face a different market: values have fallen far enough that existing loans exceed what the property is worth, and the realistic outcomes are a discounted sale, a loan sale or a foreclosure. With the 10-year near 4.9%, the difference between a well-leased tower and a half-empty one shows up in both the loan amount and the cap rate.

### Manhattan office leasing heads for its best year since 2000

The Real Deal reported that Manhattan is on pace for about **44 million square feet** of leasing in 2026, which would be the busiest year since 2000, when leasing reached about 50 million. More than 37 million square feet had been leased through October, according to Colliers. The largest lease of the year was Simpson Thacher’s **916,000 square feet** at 570 Fifth Avenue, and other major deals included Morgan Lewis’s 205,000 square feet at 1251 Sixth Avenue and Anthropic taking an entire building at 330 Hudson Street.

Pricing has followed demand. Castle Hook Partners reportedly agreed to pay up to **$21.2 million a year** for a 53,000-square-foot penthouse at 625 Madison Avenue, potentially a Manhattan record at $350 to $400 per square foot. SL Green raised its annual earnings guidance by more than a quarter, and One Vanderbilt is fully leased. Supply is a large part of the story: no major tower has opened since One Vanderbilt apart from JPMorgan Chase’s headquarters, and the next wave is not expected until around 2030. The article listed AI valuations, inflation and unpredictable interest rates as the main risks.

**Why it matters:** When availability falls and no new buildings are coming, landlords of the best buildings gain pricing power, which raises rents and reduces concessions. Those higher rents also make new development easier to justify, which is why construction financing has returned for well-leased Manhattan projects.

**Interview angle:** I’d explain that Manhattan’s recovery is driven as much by missing supply and conversions as by new demand.

### Manhattan asking rents return to pre-pandemic levels

Colliers’ monthly report, published November 2, showed Manhattan leasing up **54.1%** from September and **24.7%** from the same period in 2025, matching the pre-pandemic average. Midtown led with **2.4 million square feet** leased, followed by Midtown South at about **1.5 million** and Lower Manhattan at **567,181**. Major deals included Citadel’s 1.05 million-square-foot anchor lease at 350 Park Avenue, Ramp’s expansion to 460,300 square feet at 28-40 West 23rd Street, Seward & Kissel’s 171,112-square-foot renewal at One Battery Park Plaza and Morrison & Foerster’s 141,148-square-foot renewal at 250 West 55th Street.

Pricing followed. The average asking rent reached **$79.47 per square foot**, up from $78.36 in September and $75.18 a year earlier, which matched Manhattan’s pre-pandemic average, and Midtown’s average passed its March 2020 level. Availability fell 0.1 points to **12.3%**, the tightest since August 2020, Midtown’s available supply dropped below its March 2020 level, and sublet supply hit a seven-year low. Colliers said Manhattan is on pace for its strongest leasing year since 2000 if conditions hold.

**Why it matters:** Falling sublet space is a sign that tenants who once had excess space now need it, which removes a source of cheap competition for landlords. Higher asking rents and lower availability support both income and values for well-located buildings.

**Interview angle:** I’d point to sublet supply as an early indicator of office demand, since it falls before direct vacancy does.  
**Possible follow-up:** Why can asking rents rise while national office vacancy stays near 18%?

## Retail

**How retail is performing:** Retail fundamentals remain tight because almost nothing new is being built. CBRE’s Q2 national baseline showed availability of 4.9% and asking rents up 2.4% from a year earlier, and local data this week pointed the same way. In the Chicago area, new retail space under development equals only about 0.3% of total supply, according to The Real Deal, and in metro Denver only 14,000 square feet was completed in Q3, down from 190,000 a year earlier. Denver’s vacancy still rose to 5.1% with 98,000 square feet of negative absorption, so tight supply does not mean every market is growing.

Investors are favoring grocery-anchored and open-air centers. Chicago-area retail investment reached about $1.8 billion so far this year, up roughly 39%, led by AEW’s $125 million purchase of Deer Park Town Center, described as the largest retail sale in Chicagoland in a decade. Denver’s Q3 retail investment volume nearly doubled from a year earlier to $293.2 million. Enclosed malls are a mixed picture: Brookfield’s GGP refinanced Oakbrook Center with an $800 million CMBS loan, while Chicago’s Ford City Mall has struggled with safety problems and missed repair deadlines.

**Capital markets read-through:** Lenders remain active in high-quality retail, and Brookfield’s GGP refinanced Oakbrook Center in suburban Chicago with an $800 million CMBS loan. Value-add buyers of centers with vacancy, such as Lamar Station Plaza at 76% occupancy, are pricing in lease-up costs, which is why that center traded at about $112 per square foot. With few new centers coming, buyers are betting that vacant space in existing centers can be filled.

**Market data:** CBRE’s **Q2 national baseline, published July 29**, showed availability of **4.9%** and asking rents of **$24.79 per square foot**, up **2.4%**. ## Industrial

**How industrial is performing:** Industrial is in its strongest demand stretch in almost four years. CBRE’s Q3 report showed net absorption of 104.6 million square feet, the highest quarter since Q4 2022 and well above the 10-year quarterly average of 82.9 million. Year-to-date absorption reached 237.8 million square feet, compared with 97.7 million a year earlier. Leasing rose 27.1% to 276.8 million square feet, and leases of 700,000 square feet or larger nearly doubled. Vacancy fell to 6.4%.

Supply is the next test. Completions rose to 79.4 million square feet in Q3 from 47.3 million in Q2, and 278 million square feet is under construction, up 9.3%. Dallas-Fort Worth has the largest pipeline at 34.4 million square feet, followed by Phoenix and Houston. Rent growth is modest at 3.6%, and CBRE said older buildings still struggle with vacancy while newer space leases first.

**Capital markets read-through:** Lenders and equity partners are comfortable with industrial. CBRE reported an average loan-to-value ratio of 60.5% in Q3, up from 57.5% in Q2, which shows lenders are extending more leverage. LBA’s refinancing pulled cash out of a warehouse campus bought in 2022, and PCCP’s backing of the Northwoods purchase shows institutional equity will fund large, stabilized parks. CBRE expects $130.7 billion of industrial investment this year, up 15%.

**Market data:** CBRE’s **Q3 report, published October 23**, recorded vacancy of **6.4%**, availability of **8.9%**, net absorption of **104.6 million square feet**, completions of **79.4 million**, **278 million under construction**, and average net asking rent of **$11.16 per square foot**, up **3.6%**. Year-to-date absorption led in Dallas-Fort Worth (**23.6 million**), Houston (**18.1 million**) and Atlanta (**13.6 million**). ### Adkisson and PCCP buy Houston’s largest industrial park

## Multifamily

**How multifamily is performing:** Apartment fundamentals are steady, with supply finally falling. CBRE’s Q3 report, published November 4, showed average rent of $2,268 per month, up 1% from a year earlier and 0.5% from Q2. The Midwest led rent growth at 2.3%, followed by the Northeast at 2.2% and the Pacific at 1.4%. Only 21 markets had falling rents, down from 33 in Q2. Vacancy rose 40 basis points to 4.7%, still below the long-term average of about 5.0%.

Demand slowed in Q3. Net absorption fell to 8,747 units from 42,166 in Q2, and only 40 markets had positive absorption, down from 69. Dallas led with 3,000 units, followed by Los Angeles and Orlando. Supply is easing faster: completions fell 11% from a year earlier to 85,300 units, and CBRE expects construction to slow further. CBRE’s Kelli Carhart said peak supply appears to be behind the sector and a gradual recovery in deal activity is expected.

**Capital markets read-through:** Credit is weaker than operations. Multifamily CMBS delinquency rose to 8.04% in September, and lenders are giving troubled loans less time. Nomura’s Larry Kravetz said extensions now run months rather than years and expects more foreclosures and loan sales. Agency loans are far healthier, with Freddie Mac’s multifamily delinquency at 0.8%. Investment volume, excluding the AvalonBay and Equity Residential merger, fell 8.7%, and CBRE said the bid-ask gap remains a challenge.

**Market data:** CBRE’s **Q3 release, published November 4**, reported vacancy of **4.7%**, average rent of **$2,268**, net absorption of **8,747 units** and completions of **85,300 units**. [CBRE](https://www.cbre.com/press-releases/us-multifamily-rents-edge-higher-q3-2026-construction-slows) Trepp’s September data, **reported October 19**, showed multifamily CMBS delinquency of **8.04%, up 35 bp**, and special servicing of **8.54%**. ### CBRE: rents edge up as construction slows

CBRE’s Q3 report described an apartment market that has absorbed the supply wave. Rent growth turned positive in more markets, with 21 markets showing annual declines compared with 33 in Q2, and vacancy at 4.7% remains below its long-term average. ## Data Centers

**How data centers are performing:** Demand remains far ahead of supply. CBRE’s H1 report, the latest national benchmark, showed primary-market vacancy of 1.4%, 7,481 MW under construction and 80.4% of that capacity preleased. Less than 1,500 MW of future capacity was available, about six months of demand. The constraints are power and approvals, not tenants.

This week the money and the opposition both grew. For developers, approval risk now matters as much as power: by-right zoning, like Goochland County’s technology overlay district, is valuable, but it is also what local opponents challenge in court.

**Market data:** CBRE’s **H1 report, published August 27**, recorded primary-market vacancy of **1.4%**, **7,481.1 MW under construction** and **80.4% preleasing**.
